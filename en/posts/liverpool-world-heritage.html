Beyond "Preservation vs. Creation": What Liverpool's World Heritage Delisting Teaches Us

July 13, 2026 | Environment, Education

World Heritage is a shared treasure of humanity that must be passed down to future generations. However, for the people living there, it is also a "living city."

A symbolic clash between these two values took place in Liverpool, UK, leading to its delisting from the World Heritage register.

In 2004, Liverpool's historic waterfront was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site, recognized for its outstanding value in demonstrating the development of 18th- and 19th-century maritime trade and modern port technology. However, a major urban regeneration project, "Liverpool Waters," was launched in the long-declining northern docks, and a plan emerged to build Everton FC's new stadium at the historic Bramley-Moore Dock. UNESCO repeatedly warned that these developments would irreversibly damage the "authenticity" and "integrity" of the site. Ultimately, in 2021, Liverpool lost its World Heritage status.

Yet, we cannot simply conclude that "development was the villain."

Liverpool faced the reality of high unemployment and a stagnant regional economy. The city argued that "preserving the city as a museum does not protect the lives of its citizens," choosing to prioritize jobs and investment. On the other hand, UNESCO believed that "once historic value is lost, it can never be recovered."

Both sides carried out their respective, valid missions.

The lesson this issue offers to cities worldwide is not a binary choice between "preservation or development."

What is truly needed is a societal consensus on "what to preserve and what to change."

Cultural heritage is not just buildings; it is a synthesis of local history, landscape, and collective memory. Meanwhile, cities must evolve like living organisms, or they will decay. Therefore, rather than treating urban planning, landscape design, cultural policy, and economic policy as separate domains, we must integrate them into a single vision.

In Japan, water-facing urban designs and waterfront revitalizations are underway in many regions. In these initiatives, simply preserving historic structures or landscapes is not enough. Nor will pursuing only economic benefits lead to long-term success.

The key lies in the concept of "Creative Conservation" — transforming local history and culture into future value.

We should not halt development to protect history, nor destroy history for development.

Reshaping history as a resource while fostering new cultures, industries, and human connections is the very essence of sustainable community building.

Liverpool's World Heritage delisting is sometimes told as a failure of urban regeneration. However, its true significance lies in raising a powerful global question: the era of pitting "development" against "preservation" is over, and a new form of urban governance that harmonizes both is required.

This question is by no means irrelevant, not only to World Heritage cities but also to those of us working on revitalizing regional cities and riverfront spaces in Japan. Discerning what to keep for the future while creating new value — this sense of balance is the very capability most required for future town planning.
